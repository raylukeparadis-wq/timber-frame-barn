    Roof

    Structural check

    Checks every timber for its own weight, the roof and the snow, so you can see what a building official’s engineer will see. It is a design aid, not an engineer’s stamp: Massachusetts expects stamped drawings for a timber frame.

    Loads: snow by ASCE 7-16 as adopted by 780 CMR (10th edition), roof snow = slope factor × the larger of 0.7 × exposure × thermal × ground snow and the town’s minimum flat-roof snow; drifted (unbalanced) snow both ways on slopes from ½ to 7 on 12. Timber design by the NDS, allowable stress, with the reference design values of NDS Supplement Table 4D (2018) for the species and grade (beams and stringers or posts and timbers by shape); ungraded native lumber at No. 2 values, with columns designed for 25% more load (780 CMR 2303.1.14.1); snow load duration 1.15, dry service, size factor on deep timbers, column stability with the 50 slenderness limit, bending with axial force combined, deflection limited to span ÷ 180.

    Load path: plates, purlins and ridge carry the roof halfway to their neighbours and span bent to bent as simple beams (knee braces under them not counted). Each bent is solved as a plane frame with pinned joints, each timber running continuous through the joints along it, and posts pinned at the foundation.

    With structural roof sheathing ticked, a bent that can’t stand square by itself (a garage-door wall with no knee braces) is held sideways at its peak by the roof deck, and the force that takes is reported for the engineer. Not checked: wind, racking and uplift; the joints (tenons, pegs, housings, bearing); timber left at mortises; girts; the foundation; floors. An engineer must check those.

    Cut sheet

    Hours per piece: about 0.4 to lay it out from its reference faces, then each end and each mortise: a crosscut 0.15, a stub tenon 0.4, a 4″ tenon 0.75, a through tenon 1.25, a seat 0.5, a half of a scarf 1.75, an English tying joint end 3, a mortise 0.8, a through mortise 1.2. These are rules of thumb for an experienced framer with power tools; the setting above scales them. Raising, finishing and handling aren't included.

    Buy timbers or saw your own

    Compares buying every timber grade-stamped, buying logs and sawing them all on your mill, and a mix: saw what passes the structural check as ungraded native lumber, buy grade-stamped only what doesn’t. Prices for buying come from the purchase list; the species and grade from the structural check.

    Logs: each timber 6×6 and up is sawn boxed-heart from its own log, whose small end inside the bark is the timber’s diagonal plus the wane allowance, 4″ longer than the timber, priced by Doyle scale, (diameter − 4)² ÷ 16 × length. Its side lumber (International ¼″ scale less the timber, times the recovery) supplies the joists, commons and stair parts first; the rest comes from extra logs sized like those on the knee brace log list, at its overrun. Defaults are placeholders: enter your log seller’s price and your mill’s figures.

    Massachusetts code (780 CMR 2303.1.14): ungraded lumber may be used in barns, sheds and accessory buildings only as native lumber, from a mill registered with the Board of Building Regulations and Standards (780 CMR 110.R4) with each piece stamped, or certified by an inspector of an agency accredited by the American Lumber Standard Committee. It is designed at No. 2 values, with columns for 25% more load, which is how “Saw all” is checked. Ask your building inspector which route they accept before you saw.

    Joinery and square rule guide

    Rules of thumb for 6×6 to 10×10 timber frames, not engineering. For structural design, the US standard is TFEC 1, Standard for Design of Timber Frame Structures.

    Square rule layout for this frame

    • Every rough timber is treated as containing a smaller, perfectly straight and square timber, ½″ under nominal each way. Joints are laid out to that perfect timber, never to the rough one.
    • Where another member joins, cut a ½″ housing down to the perfect size, so every joint fits the same way whatever the rough timber’s real size or twist.
    • Mark two adjacent reference faces on every timber, choosing the straightest. Take every measurement from them, for example a 1½″ tenon laid out 2″ in from the reference face.
    • Assemble with reference faces oriented the same way (listed below), so the outside of the frame stays flat for sheathing.
    • Put the crown up on every horizontal member, and label each piece by its bent, side and position.
    MemberNominalPerfect timberReference faces
    Tenons
    • Thickness 1½″ (2″ for heavier frames), about ¼ to ⅓ of the member’s width, laid out a fixed distance from a reference face.
    • Length 4″ to 5″ into an 8″ post; a through tenon runs the full depth.
    • Width: the full depth of the member, less any housing or a small top shoulder.
    • Cut shoulders square and slightly undercut so the outer edge closes tight; chamfer the tenon end so it starts easily.
    Mortises
    • Same width as the tenon, about ¼″ deeper than the tenon is long, so the shoulder bears rather than the tip.
    • Don’t remove more than about ⅓ of a post’s section at one height. Stagger mortises entering from opposite sides.
    • Keep mortises clear of knots, large checks and timber ends.
    Housings
    • ½″ to ¾″ deep, so the full section of the beam bears on the post, and gaps from shrinkage stay hidden.
    Pegs and drawboring
    • Dry, straight-grained hardwood such as white oak or black locust, ideally split rather than sawn.
    • 1″ pegs for major joints, ¾″ for small members. Two pegs in major beam-to-post joints, one in girts.
    • At least 2 peg diameters from the shoulder and from the tenon end (the relish).
    • Drawbore: bore the tenon’s hole 1/16″ to ⅛″ nearer the shoulder than the post’s hole, so driving the peg pulls the joint tight.
    Scarf joints
    • About 3 to 4 times the timber’s depth long (24″ to 32″ on an 8×8), longer for a stop-splayed scarf.
    • Just past a post, where it’s supported. Never mid-span; stagger them between the two walls.
    • Typically four pegs, with folding wedges in a stop-splayed scarf.
    General
    • Use one layout system, square rule here, throughout the frame.
    • Plan for shrinkage: green oak can shrink about ½″ across an 8″ face as it dries. Housings and drawbored pegs keep joints tight.
