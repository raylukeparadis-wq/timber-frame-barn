    Joinery and square rule guide

    Rules of thumb for 6×6 to 10×10 timber frames, not engineering. For structural design, the US standard is TFEC 1, Standard for Design of Timber Frame Structures.

    Square rule layout for this frame

    • Every rough timber is treated as containing a smaller, perfectly straight and square timber, ½″ under nominal each way. Joints are laid out to that perfect timber, never to the rough one.
    • Where another member joins, cut a ½″ housing down to the perfect size, so every joint fits the same way whatever the rough timber’s real size or twist.
    • Mark two adjacent reference faces on every timber, choosing the straightest. Take every measurement from them, for example a 1½″ tenon laid out 2″ in from the reference face.
    • Assemble with reference faces oriented the same way (listed below), so the outside of the frame stays flat for sheathing.
    • Put the crown up on every horizontal member, and label each piece by its bent, side and position.
    MemberNominalPerfect timberReference faces
    Tenons
    • Thickness 1½″ (2″ for heavier frames), about ¼ to ⅓ of the member’s width, laid out a fixed distance from a reference face.
    • Length 4″ to 5″ into an 8″ post; a through tenon runs the full depth.
    • Width: the full depth of the member, less any housing or a small top shoulder.
    • Cut shoulders square and slightly undercut so the outer edge closes tight; chamfer the tenon end so it starts easily.
    Mortises
    • Same width as the tenon, about ¼″ deeper than the tenon is long, so the shoulder bears rather than the tip.
    • Don’t remove more than about ⅓ of a post’s section at one height. Stagger mortises entering from opposite sides.
    • Keep mortises clear of knots, large checks and timber ends.
    Housings
    • ½″ to ¾″ deep, so the full section of the beam bears on the post, and gaps from shrinkage stay hidden.
    Pegs and drawboring
    • Dry, straight-grained hardwood such as white oak or black locust, ideally split rather than sawn.
    • 1″ pegs for major joints, ¾″ for small members. Two pegs in major beam-to-post joints, one in girts.
    • At least 2 peg diameters from the shoulder and from the tenon end (the relish).
    • Drawbore: bore the tenon’s hole 1/16″ to ⅛″ nearer the shoulder than the post’s hole, so driving the peg pulls the joint tight.
    Scarf joints
    • About 3 to 4 times the timber’s depth long (24″ to 32″ on an 8×8), longer for a stop-splayed scarf.
    • Just past a post, where it’s supported. Never mid-span; stagger them between the two walls.
    • Typically four pegs, with folding wedges in a stop-splayed scarf.
    General
    • Use one layout system, square rule here, throughout the frame.
    • Plan for shrinkage: green oak can shrink about ½″ across an 8″ face as it dries. Housings and drawbored pegs keep joints tight.
